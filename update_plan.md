## The most important change

At present, the homepage essentially says: “I am a senior machine-learning engineer; here are my research, teaching, CV, and blog pages.” ([Mbolonkin][1])

That understates your differentiator.

A stronger positioning statement would be:

> **Machine Learning Engineer, Computer Science Researcher, and J.D. Candidate working at the intersection of AI, intellectual property, and law.**

Underneath, add one concise sentence:

> I develop production computer-vision systems, conduct research in AI and computational law, and study how intellectual-property and technology law should respond to emerging computational systems.

This gives recruiters, professors, attorneys, conference organizers, and potential collaborators a reason to keep reading.

## What to publish regularly

A realistic cadence is **one substantive article every six to eight weeks** plus one shorter update each month.

Your strongest content would demonstrate bidirectional fluency: explaining law accurately to technical readers and technology accurately to legal readers.

Good recurring formats include:

### Technical concepts for lawyers

Examples:

* What lawyers should understand about model training
* Why machine-learning outputs are not ordinary software outputs
* What embeddings are and why they matter for discovery or copyright
* How computer-vision systems fail
* What “explainability” can and cannot establish
* Technical questions patent litigators should ask an AI expert

### Legal concepts for engineers

Examples:

* Patentability versus freedom to operate
* Inventorship in collaborative AI development
* Copyright, licensing, and training datasets
* Trade secrets in machine-learning pipelines
* What engineers should document during invention development
* Surveillance technology and reasonable expectations of privacy

### Case and policy analysis

Use a consistent structure:

1. What happened
2. What the legal issue is
3. What the technology actually does
4. Where the legal and technical assumptions diverge
5. Practical implications

Avoid giving individualized legal advice. Until admitted to practice, clearly label legal writing as academic commentary and state that it does not constitute legal advice.

### Research and project updates

Publish brief updates when you:

* Release a paper or preprint
* Receive or file a patent
* Present at a conference
* Complete a law-school paper that can be shared
* Build a public demonstration
* Teach a new course
* Review an important technical or legal book
* Participate in a moot court, journal, clinic, or technology-law organization

A monthly “Now” page could summarize current research, coursework, reading, and public projects without requiring a full article.
